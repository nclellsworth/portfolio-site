Alongside pursuing a medical career, I also create art. Creating art helps me shape the way I interpret my own experiences and the world around me, which I find especially critical as my life gets busier and more complex. Art is my way of probing beyond the aesthetic, beyond the mesmerizing allure of visual consumption which plagues our current art culture. An ambitious pursuit for someone who spends a considerable amount of time staring at things.

I am not trying to grow my art into a brand or source of income, so my former presence on social media served little practical value. Instead, it entombed me in greed. My own greed. The greed of others. I became hungry for every scrap of recognition until that hunger slipped into my own creative process. I used to judge my work by my own standard for excellence, or by comparing it to the end product I had envisioned. But after years on Instagram, I found myself thinking about how many likes a piece would get as I made it. I envisioned my unfinished work within the (literal) frame of a post. A small number beside a heart had somehow acquired considerable authority over my own. My art had a home, and it was no longer my heart. It was a platform. A product. But a product made without the intention of being a product is merely bad art! And, frankly, an equally poor business strategy.

The hungrier I became, the more dissociated I felt from the experience of viewing and creating art. I became an art consumer, viewing hundreds of works that artists had spent months creating in the span of a few minutes. That's not enough time to experience anything. I was simultaneously demanding that strangers appreciate my work and giving theirs approximately two seconds of my attention. An arrangement of remarkable hypocrisy, even by internet standards.

So I left social media and, with the help of AI's web design capabilities, moved my artwork to this website. The irony of using artificial intelligence to facilitate my retreat from digital consumption is not lost on me.

How do I differentiate art from aesthetic consumption? On the basis of what each affords its viewer. Art provides an experience, perhaps even a discovery. A revelation. It plucks on some distant string of the wandering heart. Aesthetic consumption, on the other hand, only entertains. It engages the senses without affording experience. For example, there are many paintings and films I find entertaining and perhaps even satisfying, but do they evoke wonder? Joy? Rage? Grief? Do they transport my mind into a space I could never physically go? No. Similarly, when I think of the best meal I've ever had, I don't think of a BBQ Pringle (to which I have an admittedly embarrassing devotion). I think of the first succulent bite of a truffle risotto. Both are delicious, but only one has ever made me reconsider becoming an epicurean. I will leave you to guess which.

It is worth mentioning that everything I have stated here is just opinion and not a legitimate argument against social media (which would require a much more rigorous argument, and preferably one with citations). I am sure there are those who have found a rich life in consumption and have even manipulated aesthetic consumption for their own satisfaction. I, however, have only found hollowness in my presence online. Therefore, this website is now the sole repository for my art, unless I ever decide to start seeking formal recognition for my work, at which point I reserve the right to become a hypocrite. For those friends and family who can no longer "keep up" with my life due to my absence on social media, you can be sure to find me in the garden, stooped over a painting, or tunnel-visioned on some new project. I am still very much alive, just considerably worse at advertising it.
