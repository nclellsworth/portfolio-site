How the brain finds patterns in fog

June 2025 · 6 min read

There is a moment, halfway through a foggy morning run, when the world resolves. What was a smear of pale grey becomes individual trees, a road, a dog on a leash. The brain did not wait for clarity — it guessed, revised, guessed again, and somewhere in that loop, the fog became a forest.

This is, more or less, what neuroscientists mean by predictive coding: the idea that perception is not a passive recording of the world but an active, ongoing prediction. The brain is constantly generating a model of what it expects to see, hear, and feel, and constantly updating that model based on incoming signals.

The error signal

What travels up the sensory hierarchy is not raw experience but surprise — the gap between what was predicted and what arrived. If the brain expected silence and heard a footstep, that mismatch becomes the signal. If it expected a face and got a face, almost nothing travels upward at all.

Perception is controlled hallucination. The world we see is largely the world we expect to see.

This has strange implications. It means the visual cortex is doing something closer to dreaming than photographing. It means attention is really a way of allocating prediction resources. It means that what we call "seeing" is already deeply shaped by what we've seen before.

What this means for learning

If you are learning something truly new — a new language, a new instrument, a new landscape — the prediction errors are enormous. Everything is surprising. This is cognitively expensive and emotionally uncomfortable, which is why novelty is tiring. But it is also, neuroscientists argue, exactly when the most learning happens.

The fog resolves not because the light improved, but because the brain finally built a model good enough to guess the trees.
