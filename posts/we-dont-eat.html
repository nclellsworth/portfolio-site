We Don't Eat

On autophagy, the body's oldest logic, and what it means to survive from the inside out.
Nicole Ellsworth 2024 Poetry
Under stress, the cell goes to war
against itself.
Biologists call this process
autophagy–
the body's own mouth
devouring its wrists.
But before death,
birth:
Autophagosomes erupt
from the belly of the cell,
little glass coffins
shuttling the living
to their liquescence;
atom by atom,
the cell unspools—
a rosary of bone-bright remnants
rushing back into the dark.
This is the way of the cell.
This is how we survive.
~&~
Like the cell, under stress,
the girl learns to surrender
from the inside out.
She searches herself
in the mirror's black tide—
her body all pink and blue and bloated
as a white rabbit
drowned.
Beneath the skin—
fat—
the wriggle of maggots,
stretching her, betraying her,
scarring her.
The only way she knows to stop it
is to starve it;
so she skips dinner.
Then breakfast.
5 days, no food—
"Did you eat,"
"Yes, spaghetti."
"Breakfast?"
"I ate at school."
But fear doesn't starve,
it splinters.
Three months–no bleeding.
Her hair like sun-sick cornsilk,
thin and slackened—
no boy would dare touch it.
Stop.
You see the weight
but not the weight of it—
the sickness metastasized
to the brain, to the spleen,
to the tender epithelium
of her lungs.
Not all suicides are sinkholes,
some are stalemates–a slow
and stubborn exhalation of life.
Don't you see?
This atmosphere is so full
of her atoms, her carbon,
of us,
our bodies–this matter–
is shared, never borne.
Her surrender is not lost
it is not final,
it is food
for the skies' feasting
her decision
was to give
away
not up,
to give always,
not once
while leaning too far over a bridge,
or twice holding a gun
to the tender flesh
beneath the chin,
but a choice as strict
as a nun's crooked spine,
as criminal
as a candle in love
with its flame.
This starvation—
her salvation,
it is the reason she does not
find a quicker way to the wick.
Don't blame her.  save her
